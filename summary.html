Executive summary

What this research found, and what it deliberately does not claim

One page for the decision you actually have to make: where to spend the next hour, the next week, and the next month — and which offers to ignore.

The one-paragraph version

Of 211 competitions carried over from the reference catalogue, 31 could be checked against an operator page and 11 have both free entry and a cash prize printed by the operator. The best documented free-cash paths in November 2026 are US media pick’em games (ESPN NFL and College Pick’em, CBS Pro Football Pick’em), the final weeks of the Metaculus midterms tournament, ARC Prize and the GEMS data challenge, the Sony World Photography Awards, and Deriv’s monthly demo tournament. Everything else is either unverified, seasonal and closed, conditional, or not actually a free cash contest.

Five findings that change how you should play this

  1. The reference catalogue is a lead list, not a source. It mixes free contests with paid-entry tournaments, bonus credit and pool software. Importing its rows as facts would have put at least three non-free “free money” offers in front of you: the Tom Howard poetry prize charges $27 per submission, Papaya’s cash rooms are funded by entry fees, and HFM’s demo contest pays trading bonuses that cannot be withdrawn.
  2. The realistic free-cash edges are low-value and repetitive, not glamorous. Pick’em games pay $500 a week against thousands of entrants; forecasting tournaments pay small pools for a few minutes of work per question. These are worth doing for entertainment and small expected value, not as income.
  3. The prize you can actually influence is data work. ARC Prize ($2M, closes 2 Nov 2026), the DOE GEMS challenge ($300k, closes 3 Dec 2026) and the Kaggle/DrivenData/AIcrowd family are the only categories where a capable coding agent materially changes the result. Effort is days to weeks and the prize is concentrated at the top.
  4. Several “prizes” are not money. Fantasy EFL pays merchandise and match tickets; FPL advertises only “great prizes”; PGA TOUR’s imported prizes page returns 404; Challenge.gov was shut down in March 2026. A blank prize field is doing real work in this dataset.
  5. AI assistance is often restricted, sometimes banned. Winning Writers contests forbid AI-generated text; ARC Prize requires open-source solutions and runs with no internet access during evaluation; Prize Fest bans generated images. Read the AI clause before you plan an AI-heavy approach.

Risks and rules that apply to almost every entry

  • US tax reporting starts at $600 of prizes in a year; most operators require a SSN or tax form before payment.
  • “Free to enter” is usually restricted to the 50 US states plus DC, 18+ (21+ for some games). ESPN and MLB outright void Quebec, Puerto Rico and other territories.
  • Prize pools described as “up to” or “crowdfunded” are not guaranteed sums; Givling’s $100,000 ceiling depends on how many rounds are funded.
  • Demo trading and paper-trading contests measure simulated performance and say nothing about real trading results.
  • Any operator asking for a fee to release a prize is a scam. Legitimate contests deduct nothing from winnings.

What would make this list materially better

  1. Re-check the 180 unverified leads one operator page at a time — the highest-value work left, and the reason the review queue is published rather than hidden.
  2. Track the two seasonal windows that will reopen: MLB Beat the Streak (March) and the Tom Howard poetry contest (15 April).
  3. Replace JavaScript-only platform indexes (Kaggle, AIcrowd, Zindi, Topcoder) with per-competition pages when a contest is actually entered.
