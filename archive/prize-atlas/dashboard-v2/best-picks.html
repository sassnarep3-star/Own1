What an AI coding agent can and cannot do here

This is the part the original catalogue does not answer, and it changes the strategy:

  • Tech & data (14 rows) — the best fit by a wide margin. Kaggle, DrivenData, AIcrowd, Zindi and friends reward a reproducible pipeline, careful validation and fast iteration, which is exactly what an agent multiplies. Prizes are the largest in the catalogue (DrivenData currently shows a $300,000 challenge; Kaggle hackathons show $35,000–$450,000 labels), and entry is open to anyone.
  • Forecasting (3 rows) — the most under-rated. Metaculus states you can “participate for free and win cash prizes”, and two of its $50,000 pools (Summer and Fall 2026 FutureEval) are explicitly bot tournaments. Read that as a formal invitation to bring an automated system. Prize pools are smaller but the field is thinner than Kaggle’s.
  • Judged creative (18 rows) — situational, and often banned. Photo and writing prizes pay well and are free to enter, but Winning Writers’ rules, for example, state that entries “may not contain any AI-generated text”. Check the AI clause before pointing an agent at a creative prize.
  • Sports pick’em (129 rows) — volume, not value. Two-thirds of the catalogue. Free, fun, low prize per entry, and almost entirely luck: an agent adds little. Pick one or two pools you already follow.
  • Market / demo trading (19 rows) — mostly not cash you can withdraw. Many “prizes” are trading bonuses or account credits, and several rows are sourced only to affiliate pages. Treat these as practice environments, not income.

Practical sequencing: one tech contest (Kaggle or DrivenData) as the main effort, one Metaculus tournament as the low-cost second, and one free creative prize only if its rules permit assisted drafting.
